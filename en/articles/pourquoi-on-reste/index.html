Why we stay in a relationship that hurts

It is neither weakness nor blindness. It is the result of precise mechanisms, which work all the better because we do not see them.

By Yousri Bendiabdallah, psychotherapy practitioner — trauma and emotional dependency. Morocco and online.

“I know I should leave.” It is a sentence I hear every week. It is almost always followed by a silence, then by another: “I do not understand why I stay.”

The people around understand even less. Friends and family end up growing tired of it, sometimes judging. And the person adds a layer of shame to their suffering: if they stay, it must be because they are weak, or because they like it.

That is wrong. Staying is not a failure of will. It is the result of precise mechanisms, which work all the better because we do not see them.

Intermittence creates a stronger attachment than consistency

This is the central mechanism, and the most counter-intuitive one.

Regular, predictable affection is reassuring: it is a given, you do not have to think about it. Unpredictable affection — warm, then cold, then warm again, without ever knowing which will come — produces something else entirely. Each return of tenderness after a phase of rejection brings a relief of an intensity that consistency never provides.

And it is this relief that the system records. Not the pain: what made it stop. The person who hurts becomes, in the same movement, the only one able to soothe. The more the relationship swings back and forth, the tighter the bond becomes.

We do not become attached in spite of the instability. We become attached because of it.

This is why the most painful relationships are often described as the most intense — and why a calm, reliable relationship can seem, at first, strangely bland.

Doubt planted in your own perception

In many of these relationships, what you feel is regularly disputed. “You are exaggerating.” “That is not what I said.” “You are making it up.” “You are too sensitive.”

Repeated for long enough, this has a formidable effect: you stop trusting yourself. You no longer know whether you have the right to complain, whether the scene happened the way you remember it, whether you are the problem. And without trust in your own perception, you cannot decide — since every decision assumes you know what you are living through.

Isolation, which takes away outside reference points

It rarely sets in all at once. A remark about a friend, a falling-out with family, a move, schedules that no longer match, the fatigue of always having to explain yourself. After two years, there is hardly anyone left.

Yet it is precisely an outside view that lets you measure what is normal and what is not. Alone with the other person’s version, you lose your yardstick. The abnormal becomes ordinary.

Hope, kept alive by the best moments

You do not stay for what hurts. You stay for the moments when it was good — and those moments did exist, they were not fake. You stay for the version of the other person that you have seen, and that you know exists because you have met it.

Every promise of change rekindles that hope. And as long as it stays alive, leaving means giving up, yourself, on what might still happen. That is a far harder renunciation than leaving someone you no longer love.

What has already been invested

Years, sacrifices, a family, shared plans, sometimes a reputation or a financial situation. The heavier the investment, the more the idea of leaving feels like losing everything, including in hindsight — as if leaving meant declaring that all of it was for nothing.

In many families there is also real social pressure, the fear of being judged, and the idea that a separation would be a personal failure rather than a decision.

And, often, an older story

All of these mechanisms act on anyone. But they act with particular force on people who learned early on that love has to be earned, that it can be withdrawn, or that saying no leads to being pushed away.

For them, instability is not a warning sign: it is a familiar climate. And the fear of abandonment is not an ordinary worry, it is a survival alarm. This is where we enter the territory of emotional dependency, itself often rooted in relational trauma.

Where to start

None of what follows requires you to decide anything about the future of your relationship.

  • Write down what happens, with a cool head. Facts, with dates. Against planted doubt, a written record is something to lean on: it cannot be rewritten.
  • Re-establish an outside connection. One person, just one, whom you really talk to. Isolation is what makes everything else possible.
  • Spot the cycle. Note the phases: tension, crisis, reconciliation, calm. Seeing the pattern repeat does more for clarity than any piece of advice.
  • Stop asking yourself whether it is “serious enough”. The useful question is: am I okay in this relationship? It is enough on its own.
  • Ask for help without having decided. Seeing someone commits you to nothing. You can work on seeing clearly, which is already what matters most.

If you are in danger

This article is about painful relationships, not situations of violence — which are a different emergency. If there is physical violence, threats, or control over your money, your movements or your contacts, your safety comes before any therapeutic step. Frequently asked questions

Why do I keep going back to someone who makes me suffer?

Because it is not the suffering that binds you, but the relief that follows it. In an unstable relationship, the return of tenderness after a phase of rejection brings a soothing of rare intensity, and that is the bond that gets imprinted. The person who hurts becomes, in the same movement, the only one who soothes.

Does staying mean I am weak?

No. Staying is explained by precise mechanisms — the attachment created by intermittence, the doubt planted in your perception, isolation, hope kept alive — that act independently of willpower and intelligence. Many people who see their situation very clearly stay for years.

Do I have to decide to leave before seeking help?

No — it is actually the opposite. People often seek help precisely because they cannot manage to decide. The work is about seeing clearly and giving you back the ability to choose; the decision is yours, whichever way it goes.

How can I tell whether the relationship can change?

Two things are more reliable than promises: does the other person acknowledge the problem without turning it against you, and do they commit to a concrete process, over time. A change that only lasts as long as a reconciliation is part of the cycle, not the way out of it.
