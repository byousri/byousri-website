What a first session looks like

What holds most people back is not the subject they want to talk about. It is not knowing what is going to happen. Here, precisely, is how it goes.

By Yousri Bendiabdallah, psychotherapy practitioner — trauma and emotional dependency. Morocco and online.

Most of the people who contact me have hesitated for months. Sometimes for years. What holds them back is almost never the subject they want to talk about: it is not knowing what is going to happen.

So here, precisely, is how it goes.

Before: getting in touch

It all starts with a message on WhatsApp. You do not have to explain your story in writing, or justify your request. “Hello, I would like to book an appointment” is more than enough.

We agree on a time slot, in person in Morocco or online by video call, and I tell you the practical details: length, fee, and what to plan for. You will know all of this before the session, not during it.

If you choose to meet online

Find a place where no one will interrupt you, and where you can speak out loud without being overheard. That is the only real requirement. A headset or earphones help a great deal, both for privacy and for the quality of the conversation.

During: what actually happens

You will not have to tell anything you do not want to tell

This is the point I want to make first, because it is the most common fear. A first session is not an interrogation, and I will not ask you to go into detail about the hardest moments of your life with a stranger you met twenty minutes ago.

It is also a matter of method, not just tact: telling too early, before safety is established, can make things worse. We will go at your pace, and “I would rather not talk about it today” is a perfectly acceptable answer.

We start from now

I usually begin with a simple question: what brings you here today, rather than six months ago? What interests me first is your present — what weighs on you, what holds you back, what has changed recently. The past will come up on its own, when it is useful.

We also look at what is holding up

Your supports, your resources, what you have already been through and how. This is not a courtesy: these are the things that determine the pace at which the work can be done.

You can ask your questions

About the way I work, about what I think of your situation, about what it is realistic to expect. You have the right to know what you are getting into, and a good part of the first session is there for that.

We decide together what comes next

At the end, we take stock: is there something to work on together, at what pace, in which direction. You are not committed to anything. Some people leave with an appointment, others with time to think it over, others with a referral to a colleague or a doctor if that is more appropriate.

What will not happen

  • I will not tell you what to do with your life. Not to leave your partner, nor to stay, nor to forgive your family. Those decisions belong to you; my job is to give you back the ability to make them.
  • I will not judge you. What seems shameful or unspeakable to you almost always has a clear logic once it is put back into its context.
  • I will not promise you a result in a fixed number of sessions. Anyone who does is selling something other than care.
  • Nothing will leave the session. What is said there stays between us.

Practical questions

In which language?

In French or Arabic, including Darija, depending on what comes to you most naturally. You can switch from one to the other during the session — it is actually common, and often revealing: some things can only be said in the language in which they were lived.

How long does a session last?

About an hour. The first one can be a little longer, the time it takes to get to know each other.

How often?

Most often once a week at the start, then gradually more spaced out. We adjust it together; it is not a rigid framework.

Do I need to prepare anything?

No. If ideas come to you beforehand, jot them down so you do not lose them, but do not prepare a story. What comes out spontaneously is often more useful than what has been rehearsed in advance.

What if I cry, or cannot manage to speak?

Both happen, and neither is a problem. Silence is part of the work. Nobody is behind schedule.

A word on choosing a practitioner

The factor most consistently associated with good results in psychotherapy is not the method used: it is the quality of the trust with the person who sees you. If after two or three sessions you do not feel you can trust them, say so, or go and see someone else. It is neither a failure nor rude — it is useful information, and a serious practitioner will hear it as such.

To understand the subjects I work on most often, you can read the pages on psychological trauma and on emotional dependency. And if you would simply rather ask a question before deciding, write to me: that is what it is there for.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to tell my story in the first session?

No. A first session is not an interrogation, and recounting the hardest moments too early can make symptoms worse. We start from your current situation, and you stay in control of what you say at all times.

How long does a first session last, and how often do we meet afterwards?

A session lasts about an hour, the first one sometimes a little longer. The usual rhythm is weekly at the start, then gradually more spaced out. We adjust it together, according to your needs and constraints.

Are online sessions just as effective?

For the vast majority of situations, yes. The only condition is having a quiet place where you will not be interrupted and can speak freely. It is often the most practical option from abroad, or from a town with no practitioner available.

Can I have sessions in Darija?

Yes. Sessions are held in French or Arabic, including Darija, and you can switch from one to the other during the session.

What if we do not click?

You say so, or you see someone else. The quality of the trust between you and the practitioner is the strongest predictor of results in psychotherapy; changing practitioner is neither a failure nor rude.
