Emotional dependency: recognising it, understanding it, leaving it

Knowing a relationship hurts is never enough to leave it. Here is why — and how to regain the ability to choose.

By Yousri Bendiabdallah, psychotherapy practitioner — trauma and emotional dependency. Morocco and online.

People often describe the same scene to me. Phone in hand, rereading a message for the tenth time, watching for a sign. The heart racing when the reply is slow to come. The immense relief when it arrives — then the anxiety coming back an hour later.

These people lack neither intelligence nor clarity. Most of them know perfectly well that the relationship is hurting them. They tell me so before I even ask. And they stay. This is not weakness: it is how an alarm system works when it learned, very early on, that losing the bond was a matter of survival.

What emotional dependency is

Emotional dependency is a way of functioning in relationships in which your inner balance depends entirely on the state of your bond with the other person. Their presence calms you, their distance throws you into disarray. Your worth, your mood, your sense of safety are set by their reactions.

It is not a medical diagnosis, and it is not an illness. It is a way of attaching that was built for good reasons, and that comes at a very high cost today.

What it is not

It is not “loving too much”. That phrase does a great deal of harm, because it turns suffering into a character flaw. In emotional dependency, what dominates is not love: it is fear. Fear of being abandoned, fear of not being enough, fear of the emptiness when the other person is not there.

Nor is it ordinary attachment. Needing others is healthy and human. The difference lies in what is left of you when the other person moves away. In a secure bond, their absence is unpleasant. In emotional dependency, it is experienced as a vital danger.

The signs that come up most often

  • Disproportionate anxiety as soon as the other person is distant, silent or simply busy.
  • Constant vigilance about their mood: decoding a tone, a reply time, a sentence, looking for what you did wrong.
  • Erasing yourself: not saying what you think, accepting what you do not want, apologising for existing, giving up your needs to keep the peace.
  • Difficulty deciding on your own, knowing what you want, what you like, independently of the other person.
  • Gradually giving up friends, family, activities, personal plans.
  • A panicked fear of break-up, to the point of accepting situations you would consider unacceptable for someone close to you.
  • Repeatedly going back after break-ups you had nevertheless decided on.
  • A feeling of emptiness when you are alone, with an urgent need to fill it.

Recognising several of these points does not mean your relationship is doomed. It means there is something here that deserves to be understood — for your sake, whatever the future of the couple.

Where it comes from

We learn what a bond is before we know how to speak. A child whose needs are met reliably enough takes in that others are available and that they have value. A child who meets unpredictable responses — a parent who is sometimes warm, sometimes absent, sometimes threatening — learns something else: that love is never a given, and that it has to be earned all the time.

This learning does not disappear in adulthood. It becomes an automatic way of reading, applied to every important relationship. Hence the paradox many people notice: we are attracted to exactly what makes us suffer. Not out of masochism, but because intermittence is the climate we recognise. A stable, available person can even seem bland, or unsettling.

When experiences of violence, rejection or humiliation are added, we enter the territory of relational trauma. Emotional dependency is often a direct consequence of it.

The mechanism that traps you

There is a precise reason why the most painful relationships are the hardest to leave, and it has nothing to do with willpower.

When affection is consistent, it reassures. When it is intermittent — hot, then cold, then hot again, with no way of predicting it — it creates a far more intense attachment. Each return of tenderness after a cold phase brings relief of rare power. The brain does not record “this person makes me suffer”. It records “this is the person who makes the suffering stop”.

We do not stay in spite of the pain. We stay because of the relief that follows it.

Add to this some very concrete mechanisms: the gradual isolation that removes outside perspectives, the doubt planted in your own perception, the hope kept alive by promises of change, and what has already been invested — years, sacrifices, sometimes children — which makes the idea of leaving dizzying.

Getting out of emotional dependency

First, one point: getting out of emotional dependency does not mean leaving. Some people stay and the relationship changes. Others leave. Others discover that they can finally choose — and that is the real goal. A therapist who decides for you that your relationship should end is stepping outside their role.

Understanding the mechanism

As long as you believe the problem is a personal flaw, you go round in circles of shame. Seeing the mechanism clearly — intermittence, early attachment, the alarm system — lets you step out of self-judgement and start acting.

Learning to bear the absence

This is the heart of the work, and the most demanding part. It means getting through the anxiety of distance without making it stop immediately with a message, a call, a concession. Each time the wave rises and falls without you giving in, your nervous system learns something new: that you survive the absence.

Rebuilding a centre of gravity

Getting back what belongs to you alone: friendships, an activity, a body, a job, tastes, a place of your own. Not to “take your mind off things”, but to give your balance several points of support instead of just one.

Relearning limits

Saying no, saying what you want, tolerating the other person’s disappointment. For someone who grew up learning that a limit led to love being withdrawn, this is a whole new learning, done in stages, starting small.

Working on the root

Strategies help, but they only hold over time if the original wound is treated. That is where work on trauma and on attachment history takes on its full meaning.

If the relationship is violent

Emotional dependency and domestic violence are not the same thing, even though they often overlap. If there is physical violence, threats, or control over your money, your movements or your contacts, your safety comes before therapeutic work. In Morocco, the national helpline for women victims of violence is 8350. In immediate danger: 190 (police), 141 (SAMU ambulance).

Frequently asked questions

Emotional dependency or true love: how can I tell the difference?

Look at what dominates. In love, the other person’s presence adds something to a life that stands on its own. In emotional dependency, it is their absence that undoes everything: anxiety takes over, your worth is set by their reactions, and the fear of losing them takes up more room than the pleasure of being together.

Do I have to leave the relationship to get better?

No, it is not a prerequisite. The goal of the work is to give you back the ability to choose, not to decide for you. Some people stay and the relationship changes, others leave. The only exception is violence: there, your safety comes before everything else.

Why do I always go back to the same person, or the same type of person?

Because the brain is attached to the relief, not to the suffering. In an intermittent relationship, each return of tenderness after a cold phase brings a soothing of rare intensity, and that is the bond that gets imprinted. Familiarity adds to it: we are drawn to the relational climate we knew early on, even when it hurts.

Is emotional dependency a mental illness?

No. It is not a psychiatric diagnosis but a way of attaching, built on real experiences. It can, however, come with anxiety or depression, which can be treated and deserve to be assessed.

How long does it take to get out of it?

The first concrete changes — spotting the mechanism better, holding a limit, getting through anxiety without giving in — often appear within a few weeks. The deeper transformation, the one that touches the way you attach, takes longer, usually several months.
