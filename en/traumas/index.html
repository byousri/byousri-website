Understanding

Psychological trauma: understanding what stays, and breaking free

Trauma is not the event. It is the imprint it leaves — in memory, in the body, in the way we relate to others. Here is how it works, why time alone is not enough, and what really helps.

By Yousri Bendiabdallah, psychotherapy practitioner — trauma and emotional dependency. Morocco and online.

Many of the people who contact me start with the same sentence: “it is not that bad, others have been through worse”. Then they describe sleepless nights, anger that spills over for nothing, an inability to trust, a feeling of being on the sidelines of their own life. They look for a cause in the present. It is often somewhere else.

Trauma is not a lack of strength. It is the best that body and mind could do, in the moment, to survive something too big. The problem is not the reaction: it is that it goes on long after the danger has passed.

What we call trauma

In everyday language, trauma is often confused with the event itself. That is not accurate, and the confusion does a lot of damage.

Trauma is not the event: it is the imprint the event leaves. Two people can go through the same scene and come out of it differently. What makes the difference lies in what happened during and after: could you run, defend yourself, call for help? Did someone listen, believe you, protect you? Or did you have to keep quiet, pretend, carry on as before?

That is why the question “is it serious enough to seek help?” is the wrong question. The right one is: is it still at work today?

“Big T” trauma and “little t” trauma

Two families are often distinguished, not to rank them but because they are not worked on in the same way.

  • So-called “big T” traumas: an accident, an assault, a sudden bereavement, a disaster, physical or sexual violence. An identifiable before and after.
  • So-called “little t” traumas: repeated wounds, less spectacular, often invisible from the outside. Regular humiliation, an unpredictable parent, constant criticism, emotional loneliness in childhood, a climate where you always had to anticipate someone else’s mood.

This second category is the most underestimated — including by the people living it. No single event “justifies” the suffering, so they conclude there is no reason to suffer. It is precisely the accumulation that carries the weight.

Simple trauma, complex trauma

A simple trauma relates to an event that is limited in time. The therapeutic work then focuses on that event, its memory, what it froze.

A complex trauma comes from prolonged exposure, often early, often within a relationship you depended on — a parent, a partner, an authority figure. Here it is not only a memory that is affected: it is the way you see yourself, see others, feel safe or not in a relationship. You do not work on a memory, you work on a way of being in the world that was built on dangerous ground.

Pain can change a person, but it does not decide their future.

How trauma shows itself, years later

Rarely in the form you would expect. Almost never as “I keep thinking about what happened”. Here is what I come across most often in consultation.

A body that stays on alert

The nervous system learned that danger could arise at any moment, and it never received the all-clear. The result: being easily startled, light or impossible sleep, constant tension in the shoulders and jaw, a fatigue that rest does not lift, pain with no medical cause found, disrupted digestion. The body is not exaggerating. It is doing its job — with out-of-date information.

Returning images, sensations, emotions

A smell, a tone of voice, a texture, and everything comes back at once — not like a memory you tell, but like something happening now. Traumatic memory is not filed away like other memories: it stays raw, undated, without the sense that it is over.

Avoidance

You avoid places, conversations, people, films, silences. In the short term, it brings relief. In the long term, life shrinks: fewer outings, fewer connections, fewer plans. And avoidance teaches the brain that the danger really was real, which strengthens the fear instead of putting it out.

Disconnection

Feeling absent, a spectator of yourself, as if behind a pane of glass. Gaps in memory. The impression that your body is not quite your own. This disconnection — dissociation — was a very effective protection when there was no other way out. It still switches on today, often at the worst moment: in the middle of an argument, a moment of intimacy, an important interview.

Shame and guilt

This may be the most unfair part. Many people carry the responsibility for what was done to them: “I should have left”, “I said nothing”, “I let it happen”. Freezing and submitting are biological survival responses, not consent. Naming this clearly often changes something from the very first sessions.

Relationships

A mistrust you cannot control, or the opposite: an immediate and total attachment to anyone who shows a little attention. Sudden break-ups as soon as things get serious. Difficulty saying no, setting a limit, asking. Trauma was built within relationships; it is replayed within relationships.

Why time alone is not enough

“It has been fifteen years, it should be over by now.” We think that because we apply the logic of a physical wound to the mind. But an untreated traumatic memory does not fade with the years: it goes dormant, and it reactivates.

What keeps it active is above all the strategies put in place so as not to feel it any more: avoidance, hyperactivity, controlling everything, alcohol or other substances, one relationship after another, working without a break. All of them bring relief in the moment. All of them prevent the nervous system from having the experience it is missing — the experience that, this time, it is over and it can let go.

What time does do

Time does not heal on its own, but it is not your enemy. It has given you resources you did not have back then: adulthood, independence, possible choices, sometimes physical distance. Therapeutic work largely consists of putting today’s resources at the disposal of the part of you that stayed in yesterday.

What really helps

There is no single method, and be wary of anyone who promises results in a fixed number of sessions. Serious approaches to trauma do, however, share common principles.

Safety before anything else

No in-depth work is possible until the person is stabilised. That means: a clear framework, a chosen pace, ways to calm down when things rise, and sometimes first dealing with a situation of danger that is still present. A therapist who pushes you to recount the details in the very first session is not helping you — they are exposing you.

Understanding before reliving

Understanding what is happening in your nervous system, why you react the way you do, why it was not your fault: this is not theory, it is already care. Many symptoms lose their power when they stop being a worrying mystery and become a logical reaction to an illogical situation.

Pace, always

We move back and forth: we approach, we step back, we return. This is not a waste of time, it is the method. Going too fast does not save time; it causes a worsening that costs months.

The body

Trauma lodges in the body as much as in thoughts. Purely verbal work quickly reaches its limits. Learning to notice bodily signals, to regulate your state, to come back to the present sensation is an integral part of the work.

The relationship

When the wound happened within a relationship, repair comes through a relationship. It is the factor most consistently associated with good results in psychotherapy, more than the method used: the quality of the trust with the practitioner. If you do not click, change practitioner. It is neither a failure nor rude.

How I work

I see people in Morocco and online, in French and Arabic. Being able to work in Darija matters more than you might think: the oldest emotions are recorded in the language of childhood, and many people discover they cannot say in French what they manage to say in their mother tongue.

The first appointment is for getting to know each other and understanding what brings you. You will not have to tell anything you do not want to tell. Together we look at what is happening today, what weighs on you, what you want to get back, and we decide together whether it makes sense to continue, at what pace, towards what.

My job is not to make you relive the worst. It is to help you understand what you are going through, make peace with what happened, and build a future that old wounds no longer govern.

When not to wait

Seek help without delay — and talk to a doctor — if you are having suicidal thoughts, if you are putting yourself in danger, if you are currently exposed to violence, or if you can no longer manage everyday life. Frequently asked questions

Is what I went through “serious enough” to seek help?

There is no severity threshold to cross. The useful question is not how your story compares with other people’s, but whether it still affects your life today: your sleep, your relationships, your work, your relationship with yourself. If the answer is yes, that is reason enough.

Do I have to tell everything in detail to get better?

No. It is a widespread and mistaken idea, and telling too early can even make symptoms worse. Serious work begins with stabilisation and understanding. You stay in control at all times of what you say and what you keep to yourself.

How long does therapy for trauma take?

It depends on the nature of the trauma, how long ago it happened and your current resources. A single, recent trauma can often be worked through in a few months; a complex trauma, in place since childhood, takes longer. Be wary of promises of healing in a fixed number of sessions.

Can the work be done effectively online?

Yes, for the vast majority of situations. Video sessions give results comparable to in-person sessions, provided you have a quiet place where you will not be interrupted. It is often the only practical option from abroad or from a town with no practitioner available.

Can sessions be held in Arabic or Darija?

Yes. I work in French and Arabic. Being able to express yourself in your mother tongue often changes the depth of the work, because old memories and the emotions that go with them are recorded in it.

Can trauma disappear completely?

The realistic goal is not to erase a memory, which is not possible. It is for it to stop governing your present: for you to be able to think about it without being overwhelmed, for your body to come out of its state of alert, and for your choices to be yours again. Many people reach that point.
